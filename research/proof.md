# Structured proof (v4.5): The Mind of God

## The position

- **A worldview is a claim about what ultimately is,** and an account consistent with everything
  anyone has experienced makes it false.
- **To establish a worldview** is to show that the claim holds in every account the evidence leaves
  open (D3).
- **No one can do that, at any time** (C1), and what selects among the worldviews the evidence
  leaves open is a choice (C3).
- This is agnosticism in Huxley's sense.

## Architecture

- **The shape mirrors AA's.** AA's C1 (no certification) follows at once from T1's rivals; its
  substance lies in the further conclusions about discovery, permanence and choice. MoG's C1
  likewise follows at once from D1, D3, P-M(b) and AA-P1; C2 and C3 carry AA-R2 and AA-R3 to worldviews.
- **One mapping premise with two clauses:**
  - P-M(a): a worldview is an account in AA's sense, and every route to belief supplies evidence, a
    judgment or an imposed constraint;
  - P-M(b): for any finite evidence, any account that goes beyond it, a worldview included, has a
    rival consistent with the evidence and the truths of reason on which it is false.
- **C1** from D1, D3, P-M(b) and AA-P1. P-M(b) is AA-R1 extended to worldviews, so C1 is AA's first
  conclusion carried to worldviews.
- **C2** from P-M(a) and AA-R2: worldviews that agree on all possible evidence are never separated.
  Applying AA-R2 to such worldviews is MoG's extension, made through P-M(a).
- **C3** from P-M(a) and AA-R3: what selects among the worldviews the evidence leaves open is an
  imposed constraint.
- **T-Class does not carry C1.** It shows coverage, so that §4 can locate each argument's decisive
  element and show which of C1–C3 answers it.

## Part I. AA, restated (cited from AA)

- **AA-D. Classes** *(AA §2).*
  - **Evidence:** what a person has observed, experienced, remembered or written down (AA's
    *record*). It is non-factive about the world: "it seemed that God spoke", not "God spoke".
  - **Account:** a statement of how the world is (AA's *model*).
  - **Judgment:** a way of passing from evidence to an account (AA's *method*).
  - **Imposed constraint:** a criterion for choosing among accounts that the evidence does not
    determine (AA's *constraint*, Def. 2.6).
  - **Truths of reason** (MoG's term): logic, mathematics, and conceptual analysis.
- **AA-P1. Finite evidence** *(AA P1).* Every person's evidence is finite.
- **AA-R1. No establishment** *(AA T1, K1).*
  - Every finite body of evidence fits infinitely many accounts that disagree about what has not yet
    been observed.
  - No judgment of any kind establishes one of them as the true account: any judgment that selects
    one as the true account is wrong in some account consistent with the evidence.
- **AA-R2. Permanent underdetermination** *(AA T5).* Accounts that agree on all possible evidence
  are never separated by any judgment, even by an ideal judge with all the evidence there could ever
  be.
- **AA-R3. What remains is choice** *(AA lemma L, T3, C′).* Among the accounts that the evidence
  leaves open, selection is made by an imposed constraint, and the evidence does not determine
  which.

## Part II. Definitions and premises

- **D1. Worldview.** A claim about what ultimately is. Every worldview goes beyond any finite body
  of evidence. Examples: theism; naturalism; the skeptical hypothesis (that we are deceived, or
  simulated); the teaching of any religious tradition.
- **D2. Classic argument.** An argument offered to establish, or to refute, a worldview.
- **D3. Establish.** An argument establishes a claim from the evidence if and only if the claim is
  true in every account consistent with the evidence and with the truths of reason. This is AA's
  standard for a theory known to be final, applied to worldviews: the claim holds in every account the evidence leaves open.
- **P-M. Mapping premise.**
  - **(a)** A worldview, insofar as it claims to be true, is an account in AA's sense. Every route
    to belief, argument or not, supplies evidence, a judgment, or an imposed constraint.
  - **(b)** For any finite evidence, any account that goes beyond it, a worldview included, has a
    rival consistent with the evidence and the truths of reason on which it is false.
  - *Motivation:* for (a), James's "religious hypothesis" ("The Will to Believe" §X) and
    Swinburne's treatment of theism as an explanatory hypothesis. AA proves for its own accounts
    what (b) asserts of worldviews (AA-R1), with rivals that differ on what has not yet been
    observed. Those accounts do not speak of God or nature, so for worldviews (b) is a premise.
  - *Refuted by:* for (a), a worldview that claims truth yet is no account of what is, or a route to
    belief that delivers something else (O1–O5); for (b), an account that goes beyond some finite
    evidence, a worldview included, yet is true in every account consistent with that evidence and
    the truths of reason. A sound ontological argument would show this for theism, and a sound
    logical problem of evil for its denial.
- **T-Class. Classification premise.** *(Premise; used for coverage in §4.)* Every element of a
  classic argument is one of five things: evidence, an account, a judgment, an imposed constraint,
  or a truth of reason.
  - **Propositions** (Hume's fork, *Enquiry* §4):
    - A matter of fact is either a report of experience (evidence) or a claim about what is (an
      account).
    - A relation of ideas is a truth of reason.
    - A claim offered *a priori* that would exclude an account consistent with the truths of reason
      is not a truth of reason, since that account is consistent with them. It serves as a
      criterion for choosing among accounts the evidence leaves open, so it is an imposed
      constraint. Examples: the possibility of a maximally great being; the principle of
      sufficient reason; the incompatibility of God and evil; a disputed modal axiom.
  - **Rules:**
    - A valid deductive rule is a truth of reason.
    - A non-deductive rule (induction, inference to the best explanation, probabilistic
      confirmation, the deliverance of a faculty) is a judgment.
    - A standard for preferring one conclusion to another is an imposed constraint.
  - **Practical reasons** (Pascal; James) are reasons for choosing a belief, so imposed constraints:
    a choice.
  - **Nothing else enters an argument.** An argument consists of propositions, and rules for taking
    them to support a conclusion.
  - *Refuted by:* an element of a classic argument that fits none of the five classes. Hume's fork is
    itself contested, so T-Class is owned as a premise, not claimed as a truth of reason.

## Part IV. The conclusions

- **C1. No worldview can be established as the true account.** *From D1, D3, P-M(b) and AA-P1.*
  Let W be a worldview.
  - W goes beyond the evidence (D1).
  - By P-M(b), a rival R consistent with the evidence and the truths of reason makes W false.
  - By D3, no argument establishes W, since W is false in R.
  - This holds at every time, because evidence stays finite (AA-P1).
  - No premise about premises transmitting establishment is needed, since D3 concerns only the
    accounts consistent with the evidence.
  - P-M(b) is AA's first result (AA-R1) extended to worldviews, so C1 is AA's first conclusion
    carried to worldviews.
- **Core claim.** No argument from experience or reason establishes that any worldview, whether
  theism, naturalism or the skeptical hypothesis, is the true account of what is. Any of them may
  be true, but no one can establish that it is.
- **Agnosticism.** This is agnosticism in Huxley's sense: the conclusion is not demonstrable. The
  claim concerns what can be established, not what it is reasonable to believe.
- **C2. Worldviews that agree on all possible evidence are never separated.** *From P-M(a) and
  AA-R2.*
  - By P-M(a), two worldviews that agree on everything that could ever be experienced are accounts
    that agree on all possible evidence. By AA-R2, no further evidence and no ideal judge ever
    separates them.
  - AA's rivals differ on what has not yet been observed, so this application of AA-R2 to
    worldviews is MoG's extension, made through P-M(a).
  - The hope that future or final evidence (an eschaton, a final revelation) would settle the matter
    therefore fails for such pairs. Where a pair differs on some possible evidence, C1 still applies
    at every time.
- **C3. What selects among the worldviews the evidence leaves open is a choice.** *From P-M(a) and
  AA-R3.*
  - By P-M(a), worldviews are accounts, so by AA-R3 a prior probability, simplicity, or any other
    criterion that ranks the rivals is an imposed constraint, and the evidence does not determine
    it.
  - Probabilistic and parsimony arguments therefore establish nothing beyond the constraint they
    adopt.
- **Form of the result.** The conclusions are conditional on P-M, AA's premise AA-P1, and logic. D1
  and D3 are definitions, and AA's results are proved there. The conclusions concern arguments and
  evidence, not what ultimately is, so they do not fall under themselves.

## Part V. Objections [D]

- **O1. "Religious beliefs are not hypotheses"** (Phillips 1976). It is a conceptual, grammatical
  truth that their sense lies in their role in a form of life, not in any explanatory claim.
  - On this view such beliefs make no claim of the kind an account makes, so P-M(a) does not cover
    them and the paper has no quarrel with them.
  - If the thesis is offered as true of what religious believers actually mean, it is an account that goes
    beyond the evidence about religious practice, and by P-M(b) it is not established.
- **O2. Basic belief** (Plantinga 2000).
  - The paper is silent on rationality, proper function and warrant.
  - "This faculty is reliable" is an account, so by P-M(b) it is not established.
  - On externalist theories of knowledge, the belief may count as knowledge. Whether it does turns
    on whether the worldview is true, and C1 says that cannot be established.
- **O3. Faith as certain knowledge** (Aquinas, ST II-II q.4 a.8; Calvin, *Institutes* III.2.7;
  *Dei Filius* ch. 3: God "can neither deceive nor be deceived"). Faith is held to be more certain
  than science, by its cause.
  - Faith offered as firmness of assent is an imposed constraint (a choice).
  - Faith offered as knowledge establishes nothing beyond what its grounds establish. Its grounds
    are accounts, and by P-M(b) they are not established.
- **O4. Factive access and unfakeable marks.**
  - Epistemological disjunctivism holds that a factive reason ("I see that p") can be reflectively
    available (Pritchard 2012). Some traditions hold that certain consolations cannot be counterfeited
    (Ignatius, *Spiritual Exercises* 330).
  - By AA-D, evidence is how things seem. The claim that this seeming is a factive reason, or is
    uncounterfeitable, is an account. By P-M(b) it has a consistent rival.
  - Divine veracity is part of the account, so it cannot establish the account.
- **O5. "God is beyond all categories"** (apophatic theology). Then the worldview asserts nothing that
  could be established. Once a positive claim is made, C1 applies.
- **O6. "Only God knows."** The objection: God knows which account is true. Grant that, if God
  exists, God knows. "No one" in the core claim means no one who reasons from finite evidence.
- **O7. "The standard is too high"** (the triviality objection).
  - If establishing means excluding every consistent rival, no empirical claim is established. So
    the result says nothing special about religion.
  - Concede the parity. The result applies to every account, including ordinary empirical ones.
  - The standard is the one the classic project claims to meet: God "can be known with certainty"
    by natural reason (*Dei Filius* ch. 2), and some critics claim a demonstration of the denial
    (Mackie 1955; Findlay 1948).
- **O8. Atheism as absence of belief.** Absence of belief makes no claim, so C1 does not apply to it.
  Denial is an account, so C1 applies to it.
- **O9. A priori premises.**
  - A defender may hold that a premise classed as an imposed constraint, such as the possibility of
    a maximally great being, is really a truth of reason.
  - If it were, and it excluded every rival, P-M(b) would be false for that worldview, and C1 would
    not reach it.
- **O10. "The mapping begs the question."** The objection is that P-M(b) assumes what the paper
  sets out to show.
  - P-M(b) is an owned premise, with its refutation condition.
  - AA proves its analogue for AA's own accounts.
  - The classic *a priori* arguments are attempts to meet its refutation condition, and Part III
    names, for each argument, the element it would have to show to be a truth of reason (O9).

- **Conclusion.** The core claim, C2 and C3 in one sentence, and the scope. "We might be right about the mind of God; we could never
  establish that we were."

## v4.6 additions (exposition; each [D] or Frame)

- **§1, why the parity matters [Frame].** It removes demonstration from theist and atheist alike;
  it leaves belief, faith and reasonableness untouched.
- **§3, after D3 [D, against the triviality objection].** The classic project claims
  demonstration: Aquinas (ST I q.2 a.2), Dei Filius ch. 2. The denial claims it too (Mackie;
  Findlay). D3 names that standard.
- **§3, P-M(b) illustrated [D, against O10].** For theism, a rival on which the same experience
  arises with nothing beyond nature. For naturalism, a rival on which God sustains exactly the world
  the sciences describe. Each side treats the other's rival as live.
- **§4.1, worked example [Frame: shows T-Class applied].** Probabilistic fine-tuning:
  - evidence;
  - a likelihood judgment;
  - a prior, which is an imposed constraint;
  - Bayes' theorem, a truth of reason.

  The decisive elements are the likelihood judgment and the prior. C1 answers any claim to
  establish; C3 answers the prior; C2 applies if the accounts agree on all possible evidence.
- **§5, C2 illustrated [Frame].** Hick's eschatological verification (Hick 1960), on each answer
  to whether the eschatological experience could be accommodated without theism.
